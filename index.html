City of Hollister employees are the backbone of the services residents rely on.

City services and good local jobs are under pressure as Hollister weighs staffing reductions, reorganizations and outside service models. Workers and residents are asking what these changes would mean for safety, response times and the quality of public services.

Maintaining the city gets harder when:

  • Fewer frontline workers are expected to cover the same essential work.
  • Public services are reorganized or shifted without clear service-level analysis.
  • Outside contracting is considered without a complete comparison of cost, accountability and local knowledge.

The work you don't see

Felix Manuel Chavira repainting a crosswalk on Nash Road
Street crews work within feet of moving traffic. Safe work depends on experience, teamwork and enough people to provide backup.

Sometimes a row of cones and a reflective vest are all that separate the crew from drivers who do not slow down. One worker operates the machine while another watches approaching traffic. Hot materials, moving vehicles and changing conditions make experience essential.

Jason, Andres and Felix with their work truck and equipment trailer
Between them, decades of experience on Hollister's streets.

Fewer workers, more pressure

Public work does not shrink because a budget does. Residents still expect someone to respond when something breaks early in the morning, when a roadway needs attention or when an emergency interrupts routine maintenance.

When fewer workers cover the same work, routine maintenance can be postponed and small problems can grow into more expensive ones. Workers are asking how the City will maintain safe staffing, service quality and backup as positions are reduced or left vacant.

Experience helps workers read traffic, recognize equipment problems and know when a routine repair is becoming a serious failure. That judgment takes years to build.

Water does not wait

Rodrigo Aguliera and Jacob Gomer, City of Hollister water crew
The people who know this system are already here.

Water work is precise, physical and unforgiving. Valves, hydrants and lines fail on their own schedule. Crews build knowledge about where the system is vulnerable, which neighborhoods are affected by pressure changes and how to respond quickly when something goes wrong.

Jorge Torres at a hydrant and John Sanchez operating a valve key
Different tasks, one water system, maintained by people who know the city.

Water employees perform many different tasks in a shift: flushing hydrants, turning valves, seating covers, maintaining equipment, documenting work and keeping service moving while traffic and businesses continue around them.

The central question for any staffing reduction or outside service model is whether the City can maintain that capacity, response time and institutional knowledge at a comparable or lower total cost.

Engineering does not cut corners

Tania Gonzalez reviewing project paperwork and records

Engineering and administrative staff perform detailed work before projects reach the street. Specifications, schedules, permits and records can determine whether a project moves cleanly or creates avoidable costs later.

Institutional knowledge matters here too: knowing Hollister's procedures and projects helps staff catch details before they become field problems.

When the drains fail

Jose Munoz and Arthur Hernandez with storm and Vactor equipment

Sanitation and storm crews maintain sewer lines, lift stations and drainage systems that protect streets, yards and homes. Some infrastructure is old and complicated. Knowing where backups occur, which pumps need attention and how equipment behaves under pressure is built through experience.

For residents, the relevant measure is whether staffing and service changes preserve reliability, safety and response capacity - not simply whether an organizational chart gets smaller.

The people who keep Hollister running

Hollister's public services depend on experienced employees who maintain streets, water, storm systems, projects, facilities and records. Their knowledge is built over years and does not appear as a separate line on a balance sheet.

As the City weighs staffing, restructuring and possible outside service models, workers and residents are asking for clear information about what changes, what is saved and what service capacity could be lost.
