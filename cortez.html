The Cost of Cortez

3 CITIES. 3 FAILURES. WILL HOLLISTER BE #4?

Before Hollister cuts more workers or turns public services over to outside contractors, residents deserve to examine the record of the City Manager leading the restructuring.

Three different kinds of failure:

  • Helena: reforms accompanied by a breakdown in the employee-management relationship and a costly mutual separation.
  • Leavenworth: the administrator structure was changed so the mayor resumed direct supervision of City staff before Cortez resigned.
  • Carnation: the workforce strategy was reversed through arbitration after all four bargaining-unit employees were terminated.

FAILURE #1: HELENA, MONTANA

Leadership failure

She delivered reforms. The leadership relationship still broke down.

Helena credited Cortez with real fiscal and operational reforms, including budget, procurement and process changes. But an outside investigation also documented a serious rift with staff and communication problems. The harassment allegations themselves were not sustained as violations of City policy.

The City and Cortez ultimately agreed to end the relationship after roughly a year.

$163,887.51 reported mutual-separation payout
$32K+ reported outside legal expenses involving issues related to Cortez

FAILURE #2: LEAVENWORTH, WASHINGTON

Structural failure

Hired to administer the organization. The mayor took staff supervision back.

In 2021, Leavenworth restructured the City Administrator role so Mayor Carl Florea resumed direct supervision of City staff while Cortez shifted toward strategic projects. Cortez resigned months later.

This is not a Carnation-style labor judgment or financial scandal. The failure is narrower: the operating model Cortez was hired to lead did not survive her tenure.

FAILURE #3: CARNATION, WASHINGTON

Labor + outsourcing failure

Four union jobs eliminated. The arbitrator made the City unwind the strategy.

Cortez's administration eliminated all four bargaining-unit employees. Research materials summarizing the arbitration show that the City subsequently used contractors and/or non-bargaining-unit personnel for bargaining-unit work and failed to timely fill covered positions.

The arbitrator ordered the City to offer reinstatement, provide full back pay and stop improperly contracting out bargaining-unit work.

What followed?

$120,076 reported separation package
$37,500 reported salary during the employment extension after Cortez stopped performing City Manager duties

Teamsters estimated back-pay exposure at approximately $500,000. That number is a union estimate, not a verified final City payment.

Workers cut. Contractors used. Jobs ordered back.

Carnation is the clearest warning for Hollister because the efficiency strategy did not survive neutral review.

HOLLISTER CITY COUNCIL: THE 5-0 HIRE

Unanimous vote

Council installed Cortez. The expensive top floor was already theirs.

On February 2, 2026, the Hollister City Council voted 5-0 to install Ana Cortez as City Manager.

That vote did not create the high department-head salaries, the benefit load or the years of budget choices workers are now told require cuts. Those decisions were made under this Council and its predecessors. Bringing in a new City Manager lets elected officials shift the heat onto one office when frontline jobs disappear and services get squeezed.

Cortez executes the restructuring. Council owns the hire and still owns the financial record that made "savings" the story while management costs kept climbing. A unanimous appointment is not a free pass from accountability for poor financial decision-making over the years.

WILL HOLLISTER BE FAILURE #4?

What has already changed?

  • Community Development: three senior positions eliminated in an early reorganization.
  • Workforce: Cortez has discussed moving from roughly 201 authorized positions to 174.
  • Animal Services: City emails showed exploration of a third-party/nonprofit operating model.
  • Former employees: three former officials filed serious complaints. Those claims remain allegations unless substantiated.
  • Management growth: the City has simultaneously brought in, appointed or advertised senior management and administrative capacity.

REPORTED COMMENTS

Allegations on the record

Three former Hollister officials put their names to a complaint. These claims are not proven facts.

On April 6, 2026, the same day Council approved Cortez’s Community Development reorganization, former assistant city manager Rod Powell, building official Gabriel Martinez and cannabis affairs manager Maria Mendez filed a complaint alleging retaliation, a hostile work environment and improper practices. BenitoLink reported the contents. Cortez and most elected officials did not comment to the paper.

“This conduct has created an atmosphere of fear, instability, and diminished morale, and appears retaliatory in nature toward staff who attempt to provide context, raise concerns, or adhere to established procedures.”
From the former employees’ complaint, as reported by BenitoLink.

The complaint also alleges intimidation, threats to job security and arbitrary reassignment of duties since Cortez arrived in February. The former employees further allege Cortez made comments preferring younger hires because experienced or older workers cost the city more, and expressed a preference for hiring women for certain positions. Those claims raise discrimination concerns if true. They remain allegations.

The complainants say Cortez directed staff to solicit “large” or “big” sponsorships or donations from city contractors because the city pays them “substantial sums.”
Pay-to-play concern attributed to the complaint in BenitoLink reporting. Not an adjudicated finding.

Workers and residents deserve an independent investigation with public findings, not silence while restructuring continues.

MUNITALENT: WHO CONTROLLED THE RECRUITMENT?

The City Manager's private business appeared in a Hollister executive recruitment.

Cortez's Form 700 Schedule A-2 identifies MuniTalent as her private business: sole proprietorship, owner, fair market value and gross income each reported in the $10,001–$100,000 range, with municipal services consulting as the business description.

Private pitch. Public payroll.

MuniTalent marketed city jobs while selling the tax math of other people’s paychecks.

In a LinkedIn post under her title as a municipal services consultant, Cortez advertised openings in Marysville, California and Renton, Washington through www.munitalent.com. The pitch was not about building public capacity in California. It was about moving workers: cheaper housing in Marysville, and in Washington the claim that “14% of your California salary” that would have gone to the state instead “goes in your pocket.”

That is the privatization of public tax capacity dressed up as career advice. California income taxes pay for schools, roads, health systems and the public services residents rely on. Cortez’s private firm used that tax rate as a selling point to pull municipal talent toward other jurisdictions while MuniTalent collected consulting income from the same cities her Form 700 lists as clients.

Public money trains the workforce. Private profit shops it elsewhere.

Cities invest years of salary, training and institutional knowledge in public employees. A private consulting brand that markets those same workers out of state, while arguing California’s tax rate is a personal loss to pocket, is not a neutral HR service. It is a business model that converts public investment into private placement fees and consulting revenue.

Hollister should not hand its restructuring to a manager whose private firm has already treated public-sector pay and taxes as a product line.

Questions that deserve answers:

  • Why did the MuniTalent version direct applicants to CORTEZ@MUNITALENT.COM?
  • Why did the MuniTalent material reportedly advertise a different salary range than the City's posting?
  • Who authorized use of the City Manager's private business in a City recruitment?
  • What independent safeguards governed candidate screening and selection?
  • How does Hollister reconcile workforce cuts at home with a City Manager whose firm marketed California public employees toward lower-tax jurisdictions?

OUTSIDE WORK WHILE RUNNING HOLLISTER

$41,000 Renton consulting contract. Expanded after Cortez started in Hollister.

A City of Renton amendment signed in March 2026 increased Cortez's consulting agreement from $11,300 to a maximum of $41,000, authorized up to 220 additional hours at $135/hour, and extended the work through December 31, 2026.

Cortez began as Hollister City Manager on February 2. Her Hollister agreement makes City business the priority and, after July 1, limits compensated outside work to 12 hours per month without express prior Council consent.

Her own Form 700 already lists Renton among MuniTalent income sources of $10,000 or more. This overlap does not itself prove a contract violation. The City should release the Renton invoices, actual work dates, monthly hours and any Hollister Council approvals.

Before one more worker is cut:

Where is the workload and service-level analysis showing Hollister can safely operate with substantially fewer authorized positions?
How much of the advertised savings returns as management pay, benefits, overtime, consultants, contractors or temporary managers?
What did the City's background investigation find about Helena, Leavenworth and Carnation before Cortez was hired?
What happened to the former Hollister employees' complaint, and was there an independent investigation?
How many Renton consulting hours were worked after July 1, and did Council approve outside work above the contractual monthly threshold?
What public work is currently being considered for contractors, nonprofits or outside vendors?
