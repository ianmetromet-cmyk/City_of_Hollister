WE ARE HOLLISTER

The Choices Facing Hollister

Staffing. Services. Spending. Accountability.

Hollister is deciding how many positions it can sustain, how public services are structured and how major budget gaps are managed. Those choices involve both the City Manager and the City Council, and they are bigger than any one individual.

What is actually at stake?

The central issue is whether Hollister can reduce its long-term workforce while maintaining the service levels, safety, response times and institutional knowledge residents rely on - and whether the City has clearly shown the costs and tradeoffs behind that strategy.

201 → 174Long-term staffing target

The City Manager said the long-term goal is to reduce the City's 201 positions to 174. The adopted 2026-27 budget avoided immediate staffing cuts while voluntary incentives were planned.

$936KReorganization savings estimate

In April 2026, the City Council approved a Community Development reorganization that eliminated three positions. The City Manager estimated about $936,000 in annual savings.

$20MSewer-fund loan approved

In June 2026, the Council approved borrowing up to $20 million from the sewer fund to address projected general- and water-fund deficits totaling $17.1 million and avoid cuts in the adopted budget.

Who makes these decisions?

Hollister's description of the City Manager says the manager administers City operations, recommends policy alternatives, develops the annual budget and implements policies and the financial plan approved by the City Council. Hollister uses a council-manager form of government, and the Council appoints the City Manager.

Ana Cortez was unanimously appointed on January 5, 2026 and began serving as City Manager on February 2. That makes the current debate a question of shared institutional responsibility: what is recommended by management, what is approved by the Council, how those decisions are implemented and what results residents ultimately experience.

The useful question is not “Who is the villain?” It is whether Hollister's staffing and service strategy is supported by transparent analysis, whether tradeoffs are clearly explained and whether residents and employees can evaluate the consequences before changes become permanent.

What residents should be able to see

What workload and service-level analysis supports a long-term reduction from 201 positions to 174?
Which services or responsibilities would change as positions are eliminated, left vacant, consolidated or transferred?
What work, if any, is being considered for contractors, consultants, nonprofits or other outside providers?
What are the net savings after overtime, management, consulting, contracting and transition costs are included?
What alternatives were evaluated, and when will workers and residents have an opportunity to review major service changes?
Hollister's current budget, staffing and service decisions should be evaluated on their own facts: what changes, what they cost and what they mean for residents and public services.
